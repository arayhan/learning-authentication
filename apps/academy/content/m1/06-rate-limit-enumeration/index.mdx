<LessonSteps>
  <Step id="hook" title="Hook">
    Your login endpoint is a free password-guessing machine unless you decide otherwise. Anyone on the internet can call it, as fast as their connection allows.

    <Analogy title="The bouncer">
      A good bouncer turns people away after a few tries, and says "not tonight" to everyone in exactly the same way. They never reveal who is on the guest list.
    </Analogy>
  </Step>

  <Step id="guessing" title="Guessing attacks">
    Three flavours of online guessing, and they need different answers.

    | Attack | What the attacker does | What stops it |
    |---|---|---|
    | **Brute force** | Many guesses against one account | A failure limit per account |
    | **Password spraying** | One or two common passwords against thousands of accounts | Limits per IP or device, plus watching the global failure rate |
    | **Credential stuffing** | Email and password pairs leaked from other sites | Breached-password checks, MFA, limits per IP |

    <SequenceFlow flow={flows.bruteForce} />

    A sensible limiter has a few properties:

    - **It runs before the password check.** After the limit is hit, even the correct password is refused. Otherwise the attacker keeps guessing until they win.
    - **It is time-boxed.** The lab allows 5 failures in a sliding 15 minutes, then answers `429` with a `Retry-After` header. A permanent lock hands attackers a way to lock out any user they like.
    - **It covers unknown emails too.** If only real accounts were limited, the limiter itself would reveal which accounts exist.
    - **It is atomic.** Under 10 parallel guesses, all of them can read "0 failures" before any writes one. The lab takes a per-email lock inside a database transaction so that the limit binds even under concurrency.

    NIST SP 800-63B requires limiting consecutive failed attempts per account (the ceiling it names is 100), and recommends other defenses besides, such as CAPTCHAs, throttling and breach-list checks. The lab's 5 per 15 minutes is stricter, which suits a demo.

    <Callout type="info">
      Worth asking: your **sso-backend** login presumably fronts platforms A, B and C, which would make it the most valuable guessing target in the system. Does it have both per-account and per-IP limits, and does anyone get alerted on a spike of failures?
    </Callout>
  </Step>

  <Step id="enumeration" title="Account enumeration">
    **Enumeration** means learning which accounts exist. It matters because it gives credential stuffers a head start and gives phishers a target list.

    The leaks hide in many places:

    - **Messages.** "No such user" versus "wrong password".
    - **Status codes.** `404` versus `401`.
    - **Timing.** An unknown email returns instantly while a known one spends 40 ms hashing.
    - **Other endpoints.** Password reset ("no account with that email") and registration ("email already registered").

    The login fix has two halves. First, one **generic response** for every failure: `401` with `{"error":"invalid credentials"}`, byte for byte. Second, **equal work**: for an unknown email the server still verifies the password against a dummy hash, so both paths cost about the same.

    <Callout type="warn">
      The lab's `/register` does answer `409` for an existing email, which leaks that the account exists. Signup is hard to make fully uniform. A real product usually answers "check your email" either way and sends a message that says what happened. For password reset, always reply "if an account exists, we have sent an email".
    </Callout>
  </Step>

  <Step id="lab" title="Lab">
    Break your own limiter, then compare two kinds of failure. Register creates a fresh account each time, so a lockout only affects the account you are testing and you can rerun this as often as you like.

        { label: "Unknown email", method: "POST", path: "/login", body: { email: "ghost@example.com", password: "not-the-password" } },

      ]}
    />

    1. Click **Wrong password**, then **Unknown email**. Both return `401` with exactly `{"error":"invalid credentials"}`. Compare the bodies in the Inspector.
    2. Click **Wrong password** five more times. The sixth attempt inside the window is refused with `429` and a `Retry-After` header.
    3. Click **Correct password**. It is refused with `429` as well: the limit is checked before the password.

    <Callout type="warn">
      That account is now locked for up to 15 minutes, and the console shows the exact wait in `Retry-After`. That is the intended behaviour, and also the price of any account-based limit: an attacker can lock a victim out, which is why the window is short.
    </Callout>
  </Step>
